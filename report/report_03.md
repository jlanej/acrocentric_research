## 4. Function

It is tempting to treat the acrocentric short arms as a genomic dumping ground. They are not. They carry out at least four distinguishable functions, three of which are essential.

### 4.1 Ribosome biogenesis

The 47S pre-rRNA transcribed from the rDNA arrays supplies the 18S, 5.8S and 28S rRNAs — the catalytic and scaffolding core of every ribosome in the cell. (The 5S rRNA is encoded separately, at 1q42.) RNA polymerase I transcription of rDNA is the single largest transcriptional activity in a growing cell and is regulated by nutrient, growth-factor and stress signalling through a well-characterised machinery centred on UBF, SL1/TIF-IB and the spacer promoter [@grummt2003]. Not all rDNA units are active: a substantial fraction is held in a silenced, methylated, nucleosome-dense state, and the active/silent ratio is itself regulated. Copy number therefore does not translate directly into output — an important caveat for any attempt to use rDNA dosage as a quantitative phenotype.

### 4.2 Nucleolar organisation and three-dimensional genome architecture

NORs nucleate nucleoli. Beyond rRNA production, the nucleolus is a hub organising a substantial fraction of the genome: nucleolus-associated domains (NADs) are large, largely heterochromatic regions that sit at the nucleolar periphery, are enriched for satellite DNA and for the acrocentric short arms themselves, and are partially exchangeable with lamina-associated domains between cell cycles [@nemeth2010,@koningsbruggen2010,@bersaglieri2019]. The perinucleolar compartment is a distinct chromatin environment, and the DJ appears to be the element that positions a NOR within it [@mcstay2016,@vansluis2019].

Because the five NORs coalesce into one or a few nucleoli, the acrocentric short arms of different chromosomes are brought into physical proximity in essentially every nucleus. Super-resolution imaging shows direct linkages between rDNA on heterologous chromosomes [@potapova2019jcb], and this three-dimensional proximity is the standard explanation for why sequence exchange between non-homologous acrocentrics is common [@guarracino2023,@potapova2019,@gerton2024]. It is a rare instance in the human genome where nuclear architecture has a legible, quantifiable consequence in the primary sequence.

The nucleolus is also a stress sensor. Disruption of nucleolar integrity stabilises p53 and triggers cell-cycle arrest [@rubbi2003], and rDNA damage provokes a localised nucleolar DNA-damage response involving reorganisation of the NOR and its junctions [@mcstay2016,@vansluis2019]. Inherited defects in ribosome biogenesis produce the ribosomopathies — Diamond-Blackfan anaemia, Treacher Collins syndrome, 5q- syndrome and others — with tissue-restricted phenotypes that are still not fully explained by a general defect in translation [@narla2010].

### 4.3 Centromere function

Each acrocentric carries a functional centromere immediately proximal to the short arm, with an active HOR array, a CENP-A domain, and a hypomethylated **centromere dip region (CDR)** that marks the kinetochore-binding subdomain [@altemose2022cen,@gershman2022,@logsdon2024cen]. CDRs on acrocentrics average 242 kb (range 50–480 kb) and are largely, but not perfectly, stable through transmission: 98% of transmitted centromeres show CDR shifts under 200 kb, and 27 of 49 shift by less than 50 kb, with chr14 and chr22 significantly more stable than others; occasional large shifts occur between generations [@lin2026]. The functional epigenetic state of the centromere is thus heritable but not fixed — a genuinely unusual mode of inheritance, and one that short-arm-capable assemblies have only just made measurable.

### 4.4 Satellite transcription and heterochromatin

Satellite arrays are transcribed, and their transcripts are not merely noise. The T2T repeat-element survey documented widespread, compartment-specific transcription and distinct epigenetic states across human satellite families [@hoyt2022]. HSat2/3 transcripts are strongly induced in stress and in epithelial cancers [@ting2011,@bersani2015,@zhu2018]. Satellite arrays also contribute to chromocentre formation and to the physical clustering of heterochromatin, and acrocentric satellite blocks participate in the perinucleolar heterochromatic shell. Whether specific acrocentric satellite blocks have specific functions, as opposed to generic heterochromatic ones, is open.

### 4.5 Meiotic behaviour

The acrocentric short arms behave anomalously in meiosis, and this is where function and analysis meet most consequentially.

Direct pedigree measurement found **no allelic meiotic crossover anywhere in 66 Mb of short-arm DNA across 107 transmissions**, while 18 of 19 detected events clustered in the proximal long arm — a 2.3-fold enrichment within 5 Mb of the centromere (*P* = 0.0045) against a genome-wide null, and a significant depletion on the p-arm (*P* < 0.0001) [@lin2026]. Fourteen of nineteen events were maternal. Population-scale recombination maps, including the most complete published to date [@palsson2025], exclude these regions by construction, so the pedigree measurement is currently the only direct evidence available. Breakpoints were assessed against PRDM9 binding motifs [@altemose2017prdm9]; the single ectopic event fell 12 kb from the best motif hit [@lin2026]. The p-arm depletion is consistent with reduced MLH1 crossover foci on acrocentrics in human oocytes [@cheng2009], with an independently observed elevation of crossover rate in acrocentric pericentromeric long-arm regions across 31,228 sperm genomes [@bell2020], and with the observation that synapsis in human male meiosis initiates from a limited number of subtelomeric sites and rarely completes across acrocentric p-arms [@brown2005].

The proposed mechanism is **synapsis failure driven by structural heterozygosity**: homologous short arms in the same individual differ so much in satellite array length and organisation that the synaptonemal complex cannot form along them [@lin2026]. This has a cascade of consequences developed in section 21: without synapsis there is no homologous template for repair of meiotic double-strand breaks, which plausibly explains the elevated mutation rate; and crossover demand displaced from the p-arm may load onto the proximal q-arm, where it borders the NAHR-prone regions responsible for the 14q11.2, 15q11.2 and 22q11.2 microdeletion syndromes [@lin2026,@cox2015,@porubsky2025_22q].

## 5. Variation, population genetics and evolution

### 5.1 What varies, and by how much

Within a single individual, homologous short arms differ profoundly. In the most detailed assessment available, only 65% of proximal allelic sequence could be aligned between homologues, and only 70% of those aligned bases exceeded 99% identity; on the distal arm only 20–40% of allelic sequence could be aligned at all, with most HSat1 alignments at ~95% identity [@lin2026]. By comparison, allelic identity on the long arms is essentially complete. The short arms are the most allelically divergent autosomal sequence in the human genome.

The dominant mode of variation is **array length change**: expansion and contraction of satellite blocks, which shifts everything downstream and destroys positional correspondence. Superimposed on that are unit-level structural variants within HOR arrays, indels within segmental duplications, base substitutions, and — at low frequency — whole-block rearrangements and ectopic exchanges producing hybrid arms.

### 5.2 Hybrid arms, PHRs and ectopic exchange

Population-scale pangenome analysis found patterns of positional homology between non-homologous acrocentrics indicating that natural "hybrid" short arms — arms carrying a block derived from a different acrocentric chromosome — are present in the general population, and located the implicated pseudo-homologous regions around SST1 [@guarracino2023]. Direct pedigree observation put a first bound on the rate: one ectopic chr13–chr21 event in 13 fully assessed transmissions, mediated by a 630 kb, 99.53%-identical duplication 1.6 Mb from SST1 [@lin2026]. One event in 13 transmissions is a remarkably high per-transmission rate for a structural rearrangement, though the confidence interval is wide and the observation comes from a single family.

### 5.3 De novo mutation

The short-arm de novo SNV rate is 1.33 × 10<super>-7</super> per bp per generation — tenfold the autosomal euchromatic rate in the same pedigree (1.37 × 10<super>-8</super>), fourfold the segmental-duplication rate (2.99 × 10<super>-8</super>), and comparable to the male-specific region of the Y including Yq12 heterochromatin (1.99 × 10<super>-7</super>), a region whose complete sequence and population variation are now also resolved and which is similarly satellite-dominated and structurally divergent between individuals [@lin2026,@rhie2023y,@hallast2023]. Paternal and maternal rates were 2.10 × 10<super>-7</super> and 0.71 × 10<super>-7</super>. Eight de novo SVs were detected across 13 transmissions, all in satellite DNA, ranging from 135 bp to 6,763 bp, including stepwise HOR unit gains and losses and a deletion of approximately one SST1 consensus unit [@lin2026]. Extrapolating, four to six acrocentric chromosomes change structurally per parental transmission.

The mutational spectrum is also distinctive: CpG>TpG transitions are significantly depleted (*P* = 4 × 10<super>-5</super>) while C>G (*P* = 0.0046) and A>C (*P* = 0.0056) substitutions are in excess, signatures the authors interpret as mismatch-repair deficiency and oxidative damage respectively [@lin2026]. Depleted CpG>TpG has also been observed in other repetitive compartments and may reflect an excess of double-strand breaks with GC-biased gene conversion [@vollger2023sdmut].

:::note The implication most easily missed
A tenfold elevated SNV rate over 2.1% of the genome means the acrocentric short arms are expected to contribute of order 20% as many de novo SNVs as the entire remaining autosomal euchromatin — from a region that essentially every published DNM catalogue excludes. For a trio study, the short arms are not a rounding error.
:::

### 5.4 Where the elevated mutation rate comes from

The headline figure of 1.33 × 10<super>-7</super> is a single average over a compositionally heterogeneous 66 Mb, and the original study noted that its per-satellite counts were too sparse to claim enrichment for any individual family [@lin2026]. The counts can, however, be normalised: we computed callable bases per compartment directly from the pedigree's own satellite annotation over the same region the mutation analysis covered (telomere to the end of the active α-satellite array), removing the sequence flagged by Flagger and NucFreq, and apportioned the study's callable denominator across compartments in that proportion. Methods and caveats in section 26.

The resulting per-compartment rates span roughly a factor of five, from 0.76 × 10<super>-7</super> in non-satellite short-arm sequence to 2.3–3.8 × 10<super>-7</super> in α-satellite, HSat1A, β-satellite and HSat1B (Figure 8b). Individually these are underpowered — most rest on 5–32 mutations and the intervals overlap — and we would not defend a ranking among the satellite families. The aggregate contrast, however, is well powered:

- **satellite sequence: 70 de novo SNVs in 344.7 Mb of callable sequence = 2.03 × 10<super>-7</super> per bp per generation**, about 15-fold the autosomal euchromatic rate;
- **non-satellite sequence: 33 de novo SNVs in 431.9 Mb = 0.76 × 10<super>-7</super>**, about 5.6-fold the autosomal rate;
- rate ratio 2.66 (95% CI 1.76–4.02), binomial *P* = 2 × 10<super>-6</super>.

This decomposition matters because it separates two mechanisms that were proposed together. The original interpretation invoked both a *positional* effect — absence of synapsis and therefore of homologous template for repair — and a *compositional* effect, AT-rich satellite sequence generating oxidative lesions and mismatch-repair-prone contexts [@lin2026]. The two make different predictions for non-satellite DNA sitting inside the same recombination desert. A purely compositional model predicts non-satellite short-arm sequence should mutate at roughly the autosomal rate; a purely positional model predicts satellite and non-satellite sequence within the arm should mutate alike. Neither holds. Non-satellite short-arm sequence is elevated 5.6-fold over autosomal euchromatin — considerably more than the 2.2-fold reported for segmental duplications genome-wide [@lin2026,@vollger2023sdmut], and these short-arm sequences are largely duplications — while satellite sequence is elevated a further 2.7-fold on top of that. **Both mechanisms appear to operate, with the positional component supplying roughly a fivefold floor across the whole arm and satellite composition roughly tripling it again.**

Three caveats. The denominators are apportioned from compartment composition rather than taken from per-compartment callable tracks, so the absolute per-compartment rates inherit that approximation; the satellite/non-satellite contrast is robust to it provided the apportionment is approximately correct, since it depends only on the ratio of two large denominators. All counts come from a single pedigree. And callability is itself compartment-dependent, so if satellite sequence is systematically harder to call, the satellite rate is if anything an underestimate.

:::idea A cleaner test, now specified
The positional floor predicts that non-satellite, non-duplicated sequence elsewhere in a recombination desert should also be elevated, while the same sequence classes in recombining regions should not. The comparison that would settle it is the one already specified in section 21.1 — acrocentric HSat3 against the HSat3 of 1q, 9q and 16q, which is compositionally near-identical but sits in chromosomes that synapse normally. On the numbers here, a positional model predicts the 1q/9q/16q HSat3 rate should fall near 2.03/5.6 ~ 0.4 × 10<super>-7</super>, whereas a compositional model predicts it should match the acrocentric satellite figure of ~2 × 10<super>-7</super>. That is a fivefold difference between hypotheses, and it is answerable from existing long-read pedigree data with a complete reference.
:::

### 5.5 Is the order of the blocks the same in everyone?

If the short arms are assembled from a shared parts list (section 2), the natural question is whether the parts are always assembled in the same order. We measured this in two independent datasets: 155 haplotype-resolved short arms annotated for the CEPH1463 pedigree, and 789 chromosome-scale acrocentric arms recovered from 94 unrelated individuals in HPRC release 2 (188 haplotypes, annotated by the independent CenSat pipeline) - 944 arms in total. Because many arms are truncated distally, we used a pairwise statistic that counts only the arms containing both blocks of a pair: for each pair of block types, the fraction of arms in which one is distal to the other, reported as an *order consistency* running from 0.5 (the two blocks occur in either order equally often) to 1.0 (the order never varies). Methods in section 26.

The answer is that **the short arm has a fixed grammar with free slots.**

**The landmark backbone is effectively invariant.** Across all arms, mean order consistency among ACRO, the DJ, the rDNA array, the PJ and the centromeric α-satellite is 0.969. The rDNA array is proximal to ACRO and the DJ and distal to the centromere in 100% of informative arms (n = 51–72); the DJ is distal to the rDNA in 97.1% (n = 68); ACRO is distal to the DJ in 94.7% (n = 76). This is the canonical NOR architecture, and on this evidence it is not merely typical but essentially obligate.

**Within the two satellite segments, some orders are fixed and others are free.** Stratifying relative to the rDNA array removes a confound — HSat1A and HSat3 occur on *both* sides of it, so any whole-arm statistic conflates order with which copy is larger. So β-satellite always occupies the distal-most satellite slot, HSat1A always occupies the slot immediately abutting the centromere, and SST1 sits at a fixed position immediately distal to that proximal HSat1A block. Most other relationships are strongly but not perfectly constrained (0.80–0.88), and one is close to free: the order of β-satellite and HSat1B in the mid-proximal region is 0.637 over 463 informative arms. Mean consistency is 0.93 for the distal segment and 0.85 for the proximal segment in the unrelated set. The larger dataset revises two estimates upward relative to a nine-individual pilot — β-satellite versus HSat3 and HSat1A versus HSat3 are more ordered than a small sample suggested — which is the expected direction when a consistency statistic is read off few arms.

**The distal architecture is shared by all five chromosomes; the proximal architecture is chromosome-specific.** Among structurally complete arms, the modal distal satellite order is β-satellite  to  HSat3  to  HSat1A  to  HSat3  to  rDNA on *all five* chromosomes, and most departures from it are truncations rather than rearrangements. The modal proximal orders differ: 13p and 21p terminate β-satellite  to  HSat3 (± HSat1B)  to  SST1  to  HSat1A; 14p and 22p carry an interdigitated β-satellite/HSat3 alternation and no large SST1 array; 15p is dominated by its single multi-megabase HSat3 block. Counting exact satellite-order strings, complete arms fall into 4–6 distinct orders per chromosome (chr13 5 orders in 9 arms, chr14 5 in 18, chr15 6 in 12, chr21 4 in 11, chr22 6 in 18), with a single modal order accounting for a third to a half of each set.

**What varies instead is size, by up to an order of magnitude.** Pooled across arms, the ratio of the largest to the median per-arm content is 9.5-fold for HSat3 (0.17–7.07 Mb), 5.2-fold for HSat1B, 4.5-fold for the rDNA array, 4.4-fold for α-satellite, 3.6-fold for HSat1A and 2.2-fold for β-satellite. Against that, two elements are almost invariant in length: the DJ (median 0.33 Mb, coefficient of variation 0.00–0.24 within a chromosome) and SST1 (coefficient of variation 0.09–0.16). Our independently derived DJ length distribution — median 0.33 Mb — agrees with the 297–333 kb range reported for this pedigree [@lin2026], which is a useful check on the block-calling procedure.

:::note The short answer
The acrocentric short arms are not built from interchangeable blocks in arbitrary order. There is a conserved syntax — telomere, β-satellite, (HSat1A/HSat3 in either order), ACRO, DJ, rDNA, PJ, a variable HSat3/HSat1B/β-satellite region, SST1 on 13p/14p/21p, proximal HSat1A, centromere — that is the same on all five chromosomes distal to the rDNA and chromosome-specific proximal to it. Order variation is restricted to a small number of specific slots. Essentially all of the dramatic interindividual difference in these arms is *length* variation within a conserved order, not reshuffling of the order itself.

The practical consequence is encouraging: a conserved block syntax is exactly what makes anchor-relative addressing (section 22.1) and array-level representation (section 22.4) workable. You can label the slots, because the slots are real.
:::

Two limitations. The pedigree arms are related, so some shared order reflects shared descent; the HPRC replication in nine unrelated individuals is what makes the fixed relationships credible, and it reproduces every one that could be tested. And both datasets are limited to arms that assemble — order variation that prevents assembly is, by construction, invisible here.

### 5.6 Evolution

Acrocentric morphology, NOR number and NOR location differ substantially among great apes, and the complete ape genome assemblies now permit direct comparison of acrocentric architecture across species [@yoo2025ape]. Satellite families turn over rapidly, α-satellite HOR families are largely species- and chromosome-specific [@altemose2022sat,@logsdon2024cen], and rDNA arrays evolve by concerted evolution with recurrent gain and loss of entire arrays. Robertsonian fusion is a recurrent mode of karyotype evolution across mammals, and the human recurrent Robertsonians are the same phenomenon caught in progress [@gerton2024,@delima2025rob].

## 6. Disease

### 6.1 Robertsonian translocations

Robertsonian (ROB) translocations — whole-arm fusions of two acrocentrics with loss of most short-arm material — are the commonest constitutional structural rearrangement in humans, occurring in roughly 1 in 800 to 1 in 1,000 newborns [@nielsen1991]. Three pairings dominate: rob(13;14) accounts for the majority, followed by rob(14;21) and rob(13;21). Carriers are usually phenotypically normal but face elevated risks of infertility, recurrent miscarriage, and unbalanced offspring — translocation Down syndrome and trisomy 13 — and rob(14;15) and rob(14;14) carriers are at risk of uniparental disomy for chromosome 14 or 15 in their offspring.

The recurrent pairings are not random, and the sequence basis is now clear: 13, 14 and 21 share the SST1 macrosatellite and the surrounding pseudo-homologous region, and 13–21 and 14–22 share centromeric HOR families [@choo1988,@guarracino2023,@delima2025rob]. Complete sequencing of ROB chromosomes identified a common breakpoint associated with SST1 and clarified how ROB chromosomes form and propagate [@delima2025rob], and a working model places NAHR between high-identity short-arm substrates, brought into proximity by nucleolar clustering, at the centre of the mechanism [@gerton2024]. Nonhomologous ROBs form predominantly in female meiosis [@page1997].

For diagnostics, the practical point is that **ROB carriers are routinely detected by karyotype and routinely missed by sequencing**. A balanced whole-arm fusion changes almost no unique sequence; copy-number analysis sees nothing; short-read structural-variant callers see nothing. This is a solvable problem and section 22.6 proposes how.

### 6.2 Aneuploidy, uniparental disomy and imprinting

Trisomy 21, trisomy 13 and trisomy 18 (18 is not acrocentric, but the reasoning about pairing extends) arise predominantly from maternal meiosis I errors, and reduced recombination is an established risk factor for nondisjunction. That the acrocentric p-arms are crossover deserts and the proximal q-arms crossover-enriched is therefore not merely an interesting observation but a candidate contributor to acrocentric-specific aneuploidy risk. Chromosome 21 centromere size asymmetry in Down syndrome families points in the same direction [@mastrorosa2025].

Trisomy rescue and ROB-mediated mechanisms generate uniparental disomy for chromosomes 14 and 15, producing Temple and Kagami-Ogata syndromes (chr14) and Prader-Willi and Angelman syndromes (chr15) [@nicholls2001]. The 15q11-q13 region additionally hosts recurrent NAHR-mediated deletions, duplications and isodicentric chromosomes, and the 15q11.2 BP1–BP2 microdeletion is a recognised susceptibility locus [@cox2015]. Isodicentric chromosome 15 is formed by exchange between the same low-copy repeats, with the acrocentric centromere and short arm as the fusion point. The general mechanistic framework for constitutional rearrangements mediated by repeat architecture applies directly here [@shaffer2000].

### 6.3 rDNA in cancer and ageing

rDNA copy number is unstable in cancer: rRNA gene clusters behave as recombinational hotspots in tumours [@stults2009], copy number is lost and sequence variation increases in several cancers [@xu2017], and both amplification and loss occur in a manner linked to tumour genetic context, nucleolar activity and proliferation rate [@wang2019rdna]. Nucleolar size and morphology have been used as prognostic markers in pathology for over a century. rDNA copy number declines with age in some tissues, and rDNA instability is proposed as a general contributor to genome adaptability and to ageing phenotypes [@salim2019]. Satellite transcription is aberrantly activated in epithelial cancers and can itself drive genome instability through RNA-derived DNA intermediates [@ting2011,@bersani2015,@zhu2018], and SST1 hypomethylation accompanies tetraploidisation in colorectal cancer [@gonzalez2021].

None of these observations has been translated into routine clinical measurement, largely because the measurement is hard. Sections 16 and 22 argue this is now a tractable target rather than an aspiration.

### 6.4 What is plausibly being missed in rare-disease cohorts

Bringing the preceding sections together, the categories of pathogenic variation that current pediatric and rare-disease pipelines are structurally unable to detect in the acrocentric short arms are:

- balanced Robertsonian translocations and other whole-arm exchanges;
- rDNA copy-number extremes, and rDNA morph composition;
- large satellite array expansions and contractions, including those that alter NOR competence or centromere function;
- de novo SNVs and small SVs in the arms — the 10× enriched class described above;
- ectopic hybrid arms and the recombination-predisposing haplotypes that generate them;
- epigenetic states with genetic consequence, such as CDR position shifts and SST1 or rDNA methylation changes.

Whether any of these causes disease at appreciable frequency is genuinely unknown, because nobody has looked systematically. That is an argument for looking, not for assuming the answer.
