## Executive summary

For thirty years the short arms of the five human acrocentric chromosomes — 13p, 14p, 15p, 21p and 22p, collectively the SAACs — were a blank in the reference genome and a blank in nearly every analysis built on it. They are no longer a blank. The T2T-CHM13 assembly supplied sequence [@nurk2022], the Human Pangenome and HGSVC efforts supplied population-scale context [@liao2023,@logsdon2025hgsvc], pangenome-graph analysis supplied the first map of exchange between non-homologous arms [@guarracino2023], and a four-generation pedigree has now supplied the first direct measurements of mutation and recombination inside them [@lin2026]. What has *not* arrived is a settled analytical practice. Most variant-calling pipelines, most callable-region definitions, and essentially every clinical filter still treat these 66 Mb as if they did not exist.

This report consolidates what is known and proposes how to work on it. Sections 1–6 describe the object: nomenclature, quantitative anatomy, each sequence compartment, biological function, population genetics and disease. Sections 7–12 describe method: references and coordinates, data generation, assembly, validation, alignment, annotation. Sections 13–19 treat variant analysis from single nucleotides to whole-arm rearrangements. Sections 20–24 set out the challenges we consider unresolved, together with specific, testable proposals — several of which are, to our knowledge, new.

All quantitative statements about reference content in this report were computed for it, from the CenSat v2.1 satellite annotation, the SEDEF segmental-duplication calls, the short-read accessibility mask and the minimum-unique-*k*-mer-length tracks distributed for T2T-CHM13v2.0 [@censat,@vollger2022sd,@accmask,@mutrack]. Methods are given in section 25.

### Twelve findings that should change how these regions are analysed

1. **The short arms total 66.07 Mb of sequence** — 2.1% of the genome — measured from the telomere to the start of the active α-satellite higher-order-repeat array on each chromosome. GRCh38 represents that territory with 14.05 Mb of sequence and 66.85 Mb of N: **83% of the cytogenetic short arms is literally absent** from the reference most cohorts are still aligned to.
2. **They are built from one shared parts list, arranged differently on each chromosome.** HSat3 accounts for 15.54 Mb, non-satellite sequence 15.89 Mb, rDNA 9.93 Mb, HSat1A 8.79 Mb, β-satellite 6.12 Mb and α-satellite 5.54 Mb across the five arms — but 13p is 42% HSat1A while 15p is 52% HSat3 and 22p is 21% β-satellite. There is no "typical" acrocentric short arm.
3. **Being non-satellite does not make sequence tractable.** The 15.89 Mb of non-satellite short-arm DNA is only 4.3% short-read accessible — statistically indistinguishable from HSat3 at 5.5%. The barrier is not tandem repetition; it is duplication *between* arms.
4. **Between 22% and 61% of each short arm has a ≥99%-identical copy on a different acrocentric chromosome**, counting only segmental duplications that are not satellite-dominated. 21p leads at 60.5%, 13p at 51.9%. Paralogy between 13p–21p and 14p–22p is the strongest, and the two pairs also share their active centromeric HOR families (S2C13/21H1L and S2C14/22H1L). The cytogeneticists' pairing preferences are written in the sequence.
5. **Only 4.3% of the short arms is short-read accessible, against 86.9% of the autosomal genome.** No compartment exceeds 18%. For short-read cohorts this is not a coverage problem to be solved with more reads; it is a structural ceiling.
6. **Read length buys back most of the arm, then abruptly stops buying.** The fraction of positions with a genome-unique anchor rises from 15.6–45.4% at 150 bp to 77.5–93.6% at 10 kb — and then plateaus. Going from 10 kb to 100 kb adds 1.5–3.4 percentage points. On 13p and 21p, ~19% and ~18% of positions have no unique anchor shorter than 200 kb.
7. **The residual hard core is the rDNA array, and almost nothing else.** Excluding rDNA, every satellite compartment is >97% anchorable at 10 kb. The rDNA arrays — 9.93 Mb, 221 modelled units in CHM13 — are 0.0% short-read accessible and only 16% anchorable at 100 kb. After long reads, the acrocentric problem is largely an rDNA problem plus an arm-assignment problem, not a per-base mappability problem.
8. **Reference uniqueness is not the same as correct placement.** Mappability computed on CHM13 describes CHM13. Because satellite arrays vary in length by megabases between haplotypes — a single chr15 HSat1A array reaches 1.64 Mb, six times the CHM13 length [@lin2026] — a read can map uniquely and still map to the wrong place. We distinguish *mappability* from *assignability* throughout, and argue that assignability is the quantity that actually limits acrocentric analysis.
9. **Mutation behaves differently here.** The de novo SNV rate on the short arms is 1.33 × 10<super>-7</super> per bp per generation, roughly tenfold the autosomal euchromatic rate and comparable to Yq12 heterochromatin, with depleted CpG>TpG and excess C>G and A>C substitutions; allelic meiotic crossover is absent from the p-arm while the proximal q-arm is enriched 2.3-fold [@lin2026]. Any DNM study that excludes the SAACs is not merely losing 2% of the genome; it is excluding the 2% with the highest per-base yield.
10. **The variant-representation model itself fails here.** For the ~5–21% of each arm in which no per-base coordinate is portable between individuals, a VCF record is not a description of an allele — it is an artefact of an arbitrary reference path. Section 22 proposes an array-level representation and a benchmark to evaluate it.
11. **The block order is conserved; only the block sizes vary.** Across 944 haplotype-resolved arms — 789 from 94 unrelated HPRC release 2 individuals and 155 from one pedigree — the landmark backbone — ACRO, distal junction, rDNA, proximal junction, centromere — has an order consistency of 0.969, and the modal distal satellite order (β-satellite, HSat3, HSat1A, HSat3) is the same on all five chromosomes. β-satellite always takes the distal-most satellite slot and HSat1A the slot abutting the centromere, in 99–100% of informative arms in both datasets; the least constrained relationship, β-satellite versus HSat1B in the mid-proximal region, sits at 0.64. What varies is size — up to 9.5-fold for HSat3 — not order. Sections 5.5 and 22.8.
12. **The tenfold mutation-rate elevation has two separable components.** Normalising the published de novo counts by callable bases per compartment gives 2.03 × 10<super>-7</super> in satellite sequence against 0.76 × 10<super>-7</super> in non-satellite short-arm sequence — a rate ratio of 2.66 (95% CI 1.76–4.02, *P* = 2 × 10<super>-6</super>). Non-satellite sequence inside the arm is still elevated 5.6-fold over autosomal euchromatin, so a position-linked effect supplies a floor across the whole arm and satellite composition roughly triples it again. Section 5.4.

## 1. Nomenclature, and why the vocabulary matters

Human chromosomes 13, 14, 15, 21 and 22 are acrocentric: the centromere sits near one end, leaving a short arm of roughly 10–17 Mb and a long arm carrying essentially all of the protein-coding content. The Y chromosome is sometimes grouped with them, and shares several relevant properties — a large heterochromatic block, satellite families in common, an elevated mutation rate — but it carries no rDNA and is not an acrocentric in the classical sense. Chromosome 15 and 22 also carry α-satellite higher-order-repeat islands within the short arm proper, so "short arm" and "non-centromeric" are not synonyms.

The literature uses several overlapping coordinate vocabularies, and confusion between them is a recurring source of error.

**Cytogenetic bands.** The short arm resolves into p11.1, p11.2, p12 and p13 from the centromere outward. p12 is, to a first approximation, the rDNA array; p13 is the distal (telomere-proximal) satellite region; p11 is the proximal satellite region abutting the centromere. This vocabulary remains useful because it is what karyotype reports and much of the older literature speak, and because the bands correspond reasonably well to the molecular compartments.

**Distal and proximal.** Because the rDNA array is the one landmark present on all five arms, the field partitions each arm relative to it: *distal sequence* lies between the telomere and the rDNA, *proximal sequence* between the rDNA and the centromere [@floutsakou2013,@mcstay2023]. The two flanking segmental duplications are named accordingly — the **distal junction (DJ)** and the **proximal junction (PJ)**. Note the polarity convention: rDNA transcription runs *centromere-ward*, so the DJ is downstream of the transcription unit and the PJ upstream of it [@floutsakou2013].

**Nucleolar organizer region (NOR).** The cytological entity — the rDNA array plus its junctions — that nucleates a nucleolus. "NOR" is a functional term; "rDNA array" is a sequence term; they are not interchangeable, since a NOR can be present and transcriptionally silent.

**Assembly-era terms.** Two are now standard and worth adopting. A **pq-scatig** is a scaffolded contig that spans the short arm, the centromere and at least 1 Mb of uniquely-alignable long arm — the unit of evidence in short-arm assembly work, because the long arm is what allows a short-arm sequence to be assigned to a chromosome at all [@guarracino2023,@lin2026]. A **pseudo-homologous region (PHR)** is a segment shared between non-homologous acrocentric arms at high identity, showing positional-homology patterns consistent with ectopic exchange; the PHRs identified to date centre on the SST1 macrosatellite [@guarracino2023].

:::note A coordinate caution that has cost real analyses
The five CHM13 rDNA arrays are *models*, not read-supported assemblies: consensus units tiled to approximately the right length. Their coordinates exist, `samtools faidx` will happily return sequence from them, and aligners will map reads into them. None of the resulting numbers mean anything about human rDNA. They must be masked before any coverage, mappability, methylation or variant analysis. The same caution applies, in weaker form, to any satellite array whose length differs between the reference and the sample — which is most of them.
:::

## 2. A quantitative anatomy

Figure 1 and Table 1 give the measured composition of each arm in T2T-CHM13v2.0. We define the short arm operationally as the interval from the telomere to the start of the *active* α-satellite HOR array (the suprachromosomal family 2 "live" array that recruits the kinetochore), because that boundary is molecularly identifiable, whereas the cytogenetic p11.1/q11.1 border falls inside the centromeric array. On this definition the five arms total 66.07 Mb.

Three features of Table 1 deserve emphasis before we proceed to the individual compartments.

First, **the arms are not variations on a theme; they are different objects.** 15p is dominated by a single multi-megabase HSat3 array and has the least non-satellite DNA (10.8%); 14p is 38.5% non-satellite and 95.7% segmentally duplicated; 13p devotes 42% of its length to one HSat1A array and carries the largest rDNA model. A pipeline tuned on one arm will not generalise to the others, and pooling the five arms into a single "acrocentric" stratum obscures more than it reveals.

Second, **the GRCh38 column explains a great deal of published silence.** Where GRCh38 has sequence — 5.49 Mb on 21p, 3.68 Mb on 22p — the literature has content: 21p and 22p appear in older FISH-mapped and BAC-sequenced work far more often than 13p, 14p or 15p. That asymmetry is an artefact of reference availability, not of biology, and it propagates into every resource built on GRCh38 coordinates.

Third, **segmental duplication content is the dominant structural fact.** Against a genome-wide figure of 7.3%, short-arm SD content runs from 41.5% (15p) to 95.7% (14p). More than 60% of the non-rDNA short arm has been described as inter- or intra-chromosomal duplication [@nurk2022,@vollger2022sd,@antonarakis2022], and our measurement is consistent with that. This is the reason the arms resist analysis, and it is a different reason from the one usually cited. Tandem repetition defeats *assembly*; interchromosomal duplication defeats *assignment*.
